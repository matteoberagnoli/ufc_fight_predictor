STEP 1

The model: gradient-boosted decision trees

scikit-learn's HistGradientBoostingClassifier: many small decision trees, built one after another, each correcting the errors of the ones before it.

One tree

A decision tree asks yes/no questions about the inputs and ends in a leaf that holds a number: a small push to the score. Each tree here has at most 5 leaves, so at most 4 questions. This is the first of the 950 trees in one of the final model's eight versions (seed 0), exactly as it was trained on 6,276 fights:

  1. Is A no more than 1.5 years older than B? age_gap ≤ 1.56,276 fights
    1. yesIs A rated at least 3.5 Elo points below B? elo ≤ −3.53,846
      1. yesDo strikes at range land on A at least 0.7 points less often than on B? dist_strikes_hit_rate_against ≤ −0.0071,732
        1. yesleaf +0.0034543
        2. noleaf −0.00341,189
      2. noleaf +0.00612,114
    2. noIs A rated at most 35.7 Elo points above B? elo ≤ 35.72,430
      1. yesleaf −0.00681,609
      2. noleaf +0.0005821

Leaf values are in log-odds (step 2), positive towards A. Read as a rule: a fighter who is not clearly older and not clearly lower rated gets a small push (+0.0061, about 0.15 percentage points); a clearly older fighter without a clear rating edge gets a small push the other way. Each tree is deliberately weak. The question that splits first, the age gap, and the one that follows, the Elo gap, are the model's two strongest inputs (section 9). A blank input follows a fixed branch: the one learned in training when that input had blanks among the training fights, otherwise the branch with more fights. In this tree that is "no" at the first three questions and "yes" at the fourth.

Adding up the trees

F(x) = F0 + η MΣm = 1 wm(x)

F(x)

F0
The starting score: the log-odds of the share of training fights corner A won. A won 49.1% of the 6,276 fights, so F0 = ln(0.491 / 0.509) = −0.038.
wm(x)

The learning rate, 0.0113: each tree's correction is scaled down to about 1% of its full size. The leaves shown above already include it.
M
The number of trees, 950.
  • Smaller η: each tree fixes less of the remaining error, so more trees are needed. η and M trade off (halve η and roughly twice the trees are needed), so they are tuned together.
  • More trees: finer detail, until they start fitting the noise in the training fights.

How each tree is grown

Each new tree is fitted to what the trees before it still get wrong. "Wrong" is measured with log loss, the same measure the model is judged by (section 6). For training fight i:

ℓi = −[ yi ln pi + (1 − yi) ln(1 − pi) ]
gi = pi − yi,    hi = pi (1 − pi)
yi
1 if corner A won training fight i, 0 if not.
pi
The probability the trees built so far give that fight, σ(F(xi)).
gi
The gradient of the loss with respect to the score: how wrong the current prediction is, and which way. A won but the model says 30%: g = 0.3 − 1 = −0.7.
hi
The second derivative (curvature): 0.25 at 50%, small for confident predictions. It says how far a step in the score can be trusted.
w = − GH + λ
Gain = GL2HL + λ + GR2HR + λ − (GL + GR)2HL + HR + λ
G, H
The sums of g and h over the training fights in a leaf.
w
The leaf's value: one Newton step, the leaf's total error divided by its total curvature. It is then multiplied by η.
L, R
The two children a candidate split would create.
Gain
How much the split would lower the loss, in the same second-order approximation. Larger is better.
λ
L2 regularisation, 0.0034: it pulls leaf values towards zero.
  • The tree grows best-first. It starts as one leaf holding every training fight, finds the best allowed split of each leaf, splits the leaf whose best split has the largest gain, and repeats until there are 5 leaves or no split is allowed.
  • Larger λ: smaller leaf values, most for leaves with little curvature. A leaf of 28 fights near 50% has H ≈ 7, so λ = 0.0034 barely matters: the tuning switched it almost off.
  • min_samples_leaf = 28: no split may leave fewer than 28 training fights in a child, so every correction rests on at least 28 fights.
  • max_features = 0.546: each split may only look at a random 22 of the 40 inputs (0.546 × 40, rounded up). Trees then differ from each other, and the model depends on its random seed (step 4).

Bins and blanks

Bins. Before training, each input is cut into at most 255 bins at quantiles of the training data, and splits are only tried at bin edges. Training is fast, and a single extreme value can't drag a threshold.

Blanks. A 256th bin holds blank values. At every split the tree tries sending the blanks left and right and keeps whichever gives the larger gain; a new fight with a blank follows that learned direction. If an input had no blanks at all in training, a blank follows the child that held more training fights. This is how debutants are handled: their blank inputs are never filled in.

Early stopping is off. The library's built-in stopping rule would hold out a random 10% of the training fights, mixing years. The number of trees is tuned instead.

The first six were found by the tuning search (section 7). Together they describe many tiny trees with a small learning rate: each tree is a simple rule, and the model is their sum.

Why this model

It is the same family as XGBoost and LightGBM, it handles blanks natively, and it needs nothing beyond scikit-learn. Two alternatives were tried with the same tuning and cross-validation: XGBoost was better in 0 of 8 seeds, and logistic regression, a straight-line model, came within 0.0011 in log loss. A 50/50 blend of the trees and the logistic regression passed the decision rule narrowly, but one interpretable model was preferred. How close the straight-line model came is a first sign that most of the signal adds up input by input, which the interaction analysis confirms (section 9).

STEP 3

Both corners: the same fight asked twice

Which fighter is corner A is a coin flip. The model's answer must not depend on it.

A tree that learns "age gap at most 1.5 years: push towards A" is not forced to learn the mirror rule "age gap at least −1.5 years: push towards B". It learns whichever the training fights happened to support. Asked one way only, the first version of the model picked a different winner in 34.8% of validation fights depending on which corner the coin flip had chosen.

So every fight is asked twice, once as given and once mirrored:

The seed spread is not the uncertainty. The 55.1–57.8% range shows the randomness of the trees. Retraining on a different sample of the same years moves a prediction far more: in the project review, 40 refits on resampled fights put this fight's Volkanovski probability anywhere from about 33% to 52% (90% range).

STEP 5

Calibration: one stretch

When the model says 70% for a group of fights, fighter A should win about 70% of them. One number, the stretch a, adjusts the percentages so that holds.

p = σ( a · logit p̄ )
logit p̄ = ln p̄1 − p̄
p̄
The average of step 4.
a
The stretch: 1.235 in the final model.
p
The final probability that corner A wins.
  • a > 1 makes the percentages bolder (further from 50%), a < 1 more cautious, a = 1 changes nothing.
  • There is no shift term. A shift would favour whoever sits in corner A, so without one the two corners still add up to 100%.
  • The order of predictions and the 50% point stay where they are, so no pick changes. Accuracy and AUC can't move; only log loss can.

How a is fitted. It is the value that gives the lowest log loss on out-of-fold predictions: each prediction comes from a model that never saw that fight's year (section 7). Predictions on a model's own training fights look more confident and more correct than they are, so they would teach the stretch the wrong lesson.

SECTION 6

How it is judged

Always on fights the model never trained on. Men's fights are reported first, because they are what the project is for; the model trains on men's and women's fights together, which beat men's-only training in 8 of 8 seeds when it was tested on an earlier version.

PeriodDatesFightsRole

Three measures

Accuracy = 1N  Σi [ (pi > 0.5) = yi ]
Log loss = −1N  Σi [ yi ln pi + (1 − yi) ln(1 − pi) ]
AUC = 1Nwon Nlost  Σi won  Σj lost [ pi > pj ]
pi, yi
The predicted probability that A wins fight i, and 1 if A won, 0 if not.
[ … ]
1 when the statement is true, 0 when false. A 50% call counts as half right; a tie in the AUC counts half.
Accuracy
Share of fights where the favourite won. Easy to read, but a 51% call and a 90% call count the same.
Log loss
Average surprise at what happened. Lower is better. A coin flip scores ln 2 = 0.693. A 90% call costs 0.105 if right and 2.303 if wrong, so confident mistakes cost most.
AUC
Take one fight A won and one A lost: the share of such pairs where the won fight got the higher probability. 0.5 is a coin flip, 1 is perfect ranking.
  • Log loss is the headline. It uses the full probability of every fight, while accuracy only moves when a fight crosses 50%, so it changes in lumps and different models often tie.

Is a difference real?

di = ℓinew − ℓiold,    SE = sd√N
z = d̄SE,    p-value = 2 [1 − Φ(|z|)]
di
Fight i's log loss under the new predictions minus under the old ones. Both are scored on the same fights, so how hard each fight was cancels out.
d̄, sd
The mean and the standard deviation of the di. A negative mean means the new set is better.
SE
Standard error: how much the mean would wobble with a different sample of N fights.
Φ
The standard normal distribution. The p-value is the chance of a difference at least this large if the two sets were equally good.
  • For picks, McNemar's test counts only the fights where the two sets disagree: how many the new set fixed and how many it broke. An even split means noise.
  • The decision rule for any change: better in at least 7 of 8 seeds in cross-validation and not worse on validation. The paired test says how much weight a result deserves.

The search for the six model settings

Optuna's TPE sampler (tree-structured Parzen estimator) does the search. Trial 0 is the hand-chosen starting point: 300 trees, learning rate 0.05, 15 leaves, at least 20 fights per leaf, λ = 1, every input at every split. Until 10 trials have finished, settings are drawn at random from the ranges. After that, each new trial works like this:

  1. Rank the finished trials by cross-validated log loss. The best 10% (at most 25) are "good", the rest are "others".
  2. For each setting, fit a smooth density to the values the good trials used, l(θ), and one to the others' values, g(θ).
  3. Draw 24 candidates from l(θ) and run the one with the largest ratio l(θ) / g(θ): settings that look like past winners and unlike past losers.

The sampler's seed is fixed (1926), so the search can be repeated exactly. Each trial was scored on 3 model seeds.

SECTION 8

The final model, and fights not yet fought

After the test look, the same frozen recipe was refitted on every decided fight from 2014 to the last event in the data, 10 October 2026: 6,276 fights. The α values were refitted on the same fights; the Elo settings, the six model settings and the eight seeds are unchanged; the stretch is 1.235 from the test look. Its own score can't be measured, because no unseen fights are left: the test look is the honest estimate of how well this recipe predicts.

Predicting an upcoming fight. The bout is added to the history as a fight with no statistics and no result, dated on fight night. The normal pipeline then builds its row from earlier fights only, exactly as for every training row; a fight with no result moves no rating and no average. Bouts that share a fighter are built in separate passes, so one can't count as the other's previous fight. A fighter with no UFC fight can't be predicted, because there is no profile to read. A test removes a real 2024 fight from the data, adds it back as "upcoming" on the same night, and checks that its 40 inputs come out identical.

SECTION 9

Explaining a prediction

SHAP splits a prediction into one piece per input: how far each input pushed it, and towards which fighter. It never looks at who won, so it can be used on any fight.

What is explained

SHAP explains a score, so the quantity explained is the two-corner, eight-seed score:

S(x) = 18 7Σs = 0 12 [ Fs(x) − Fs(x̃) ]
Fs
The score of the model trained with seed s.
x, x̃
The fight as given and with the corners swapped.
S(x)
The explained score, in log-odds, positive towards A.
  • The corner lean cancels, so a fight with no information scores exactly 0: the baseline is a 50–50 fight.
  • S is not exactly the logit of p̄, because the predictor averages probabilities while SHAP explains scores. On validation σ(S) stayed within 1.9 points of p̄ and picked the same winner in every fight. The stretch would multiply every piece by a, so it is left out.

Shapley values

φi =  ΣT ⊆ N ∖ {i} |T|! (n − |T| − 1)!n! [ v(T ∪ {i}) − v(T) ]
N, n
The set of inputs, n = 40.
T
A set of other inputs, those "already known".
v(T)
The expected score when only the inputs in T are known. For trees it is computed by following the fight down each tree; where a split asks about an input outside T, the fight goes down both branches, weighted by the share of training fights that went each way.
the fraction
The share of orderings of all 40 inputs in which exactly the inputs in T come before input i.
φi
Input i's average extra push when it joins the others, over every order in which the inputs could be revealed.
  • The pieces add up exactly: S(x) = φ0 + Σ φi, with φ0 = 0 here.
  • An input the model never uses gets 0; two inputs that act identically get equal credit.
  • The sum over all subsets looks impossible (239 of them), but for trees it can be computed exactly and quickly (TreeExplainer). Each piece is computed for the fight as given and as mirrored, combined like the score, and averaged over the eight seeds.
  • Units are log-odds: near 50%, a piece of 0.1 is about 2.5 percentage points.

Training and validation agree within 0.015 for every family, and a second method agrees too: scrambling one family's columns across fights on validation and measuring how much log loss rises (permutation importance) ranks the top five families in the same order. The scrambling has to be done on fights the model never trained on; on its own training fights it also measures memorised noise.

The shapes. Elo and the age gap do most of the work. Elo is close to a straight line, about 0.25 log-odds per 100 rating points (6 percentage points), up to about ±0.6. The age gap is close to a straight line too, about 0.045 log-odds per year (1 point), flattening beyond about 10 years. The fight statistics are ramps that level off: beyond a few points of difference, more adds nothing. A blank input pushes neither way. The model favours the fighter who is higher rated, younger, harder to hit, and attempts more takedowns and submissions.

Interactions

A Shapley value can be split once more: into the input's push on its own (its main effect) and the extra push it gets only because of another input's value. This is the A × B term of a factorial experiment, computed for every fight and every pair.

The model is mostly additive. The strongest pairs come in two patterns. All 15 strongest on validation are among the 20 strongest on training, with the same pattern:

  • Overlap. When both inputs favour the same fighter, the pair takes some of the edge back, so the same evidence isn't counted twice. Elo combined with the age gap is the strongest (worth 1 point or more in 19% of fights), then Elo with head defence, takedown attempts, control time and accuracy at range. Elo summarises results that those same skills helped earn.
  • Reinforcing. When both favour the same fighter, the pair adds to the edge. The clearest is accuracy at range combined with head defence: a fighter who is both much more accurate at range and much harder to hit in the head gets about 1.2 extra points. Body accuracy with body defence works the same way (1 point or more in 5% of fights), and reach with time spent being controlled (2%).

In the UFC 333 example the largest pair is Elo with win rate, +0.051 towards Evloev: the two inputs point at different fighters, and the pair softens Volkanovski's Elo edge.

Three reading rules. SHAP explains the model, not the fight: a large piece says how the model weighed a number, not that the skill caused the result. Inputs that overlap share credit, which is why families are added up. And every input is an A − B gap, so the model can say "the accuracy gap counts more when the defence gap is large too" but never "A's attack against B's defence": it is never given that combination.
